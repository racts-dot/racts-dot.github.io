Interest rates are going up, not down, and shares are falling on it

Futures point to the ASX 200 opening 0.7 per cent lower on Monday, after a sell-off that has already taken the index down nearly 4 per cent this month and wiped out its gain for the year. Traders are positioning for three more rate rises from the Reserve Bank. In the US, Bank of America economists reaffirmed that the Federal Reserve will lift rates again in October and December, and its strategists said investors should be ready for the Fed to go above 5 per cent. The Fed's chair, Kevin Warsh, raised rates last week and said inflation has been too high for too long.

Why it counts

Higher rates mean mortgage payments rise and households cut the small discretionary buys first, which is exactly where printables and second-hand goods sit. Two of the three sources are forecasts or one columnist's argument, so treat the number of rises as expectation, not fact.

ELI5

Borrowing money is getting dearer, so people will have less spare cash for little treats.

The case that AI lifts the economy's speed limit

A markets columnist argues the rules investors have used since the financial crisis are being rewritten. For years the accepted view was secular stagnation, with about 2 per cent growth the best a mature economy could expect. The piece says a serious case is now emerging that AI could deliver a sustained acceleration in US productivity, and that investors sticking to the old playbook risk being left behind.

Why it counts

This is the optimistic half of the same argument the rates story attacks: whether AI actually makes work cheaper and faster, or just costs a lot to build. It matters to you because your whole operation is one person plus AI tools. This is an opinion column making a case, not a measurement.

ELI5

Some people think robots will make everyone's work much faster, so the whole country earns more.

Anthropic is putting its first Australian data centre in regional Queensland

Anthropic, the San Francisco company that makes Claude, has signed a long-term lease at the Western Downs Digital Park, north-west of Brisbane. Singapore's Zerra DC is proposing to build a $30 billion data centre on the site. The local reception has been cautious, with the region weighing jobs against the use of farming land.

Why it counts

The AI tool you work with every day is buying Australian capacity, which usually means faster and more reliable service here. It is also the concrete version of the data-centre building boom that economists say keeps interest rates high, so the two lead stories above are linked.

ELI5

The company that makes Claude is building a huge computer shed on a Queensland farm.

PoliticsSeveral sources

Albanese wants global AI rules, and says copyright can be settled

The Prime Minister, speaking at Apple's California headquarters, said Australia would join like-minded countries pushing for a global framework that keeps people in control of AI. That puts him at odds with Donald Trump, who has rejected regulating it. He said he is confident a way through can be found on copyright, and is taking the under-16s social media ban and a digital duty of care proposal to the United Nations General Assembly in New York.

Why it counts

Copyright rules decide whether AI-generated artwork and text can be sold, which is the ground your listings stand on. The under-16s ban reshapes which social platforms are worth marketing on. Nothing here is law yet; it is a position being argued overseas.

ELI5

Australia's leader wants countries to agree rules so people, not computers, stay in charge.

PropertyNews report

More homes went to auction, but fewer of them sold

Scheduled auctions in the week to Saturday rose to 1832 from 1585 the week before, according to Cotality data released on Sunday. The preliminary clearance rate across the combined capital cities slipped to 54 per cent from 58.5 per cent. In Melbourne and Sydney, buyers who can see prices have weakened are still competing hard for the better homes.

Why it counts

A clearance rate in the mid-50s means roughly half the homes offered did not sell, which is a weak market. When house prices fall and rates rise together, households feel poorer and spend less on non-essentials. This is one week's preliminary figure and it is usually revised.

ELI5

More houses were up for sale, and fewer people bought them.

CompaniesNews report

BYD cut $7000 off its cheapest electric car and denies starting a price war

BYD dropped the price of its Atto 1 electric vehicle by nearly $7000, to $19,900. The company's Australian chief operating officer, Stephen Collins, said it was a normal campaign to lift sales of that model rather than a price war. Beijing has warned Chinese carmakers against price wars.

Why it counts

It is a clear reading of how Chinese manufacturers behave when demand softens: they cut hard and fast on price. That is the same pressure that sits under cheap goods on eBay and Temu, and it is why competing on price against them rarely works. Discounting on this scale is also a sign consumers are not spending freely.

ELI5

A car company made its cheapest car much cheaper to get people buying again.

PoliticsNews report

Regional exporters say migration cuts would hit them hardest

Big employers outside the cities say they depend on foreign workers and reject One Nation's claim that unemployed Australians would take those jobs instead. Among them is Roger Fletcher, 80, who built Fletcher International, a large meat exporter, around his works at Dubbo. The concern covers both Labor's immigration cuts and One Nation's larger proposals.

Why it counts

This is last week's migration fight seen from the employers' side. Fewer workers in food and export businesses means higher costs and fewer customers in country towns. The article reports what those employers say, so it is one interested group's case.

ELI5

Farms and meatworks say that without workers from overseas, the work does not get done.

WorldNews report

Trump and Xi meet this week, with trade and AI on the table

The US and Chinese presidents meet for the second time this year. Reporters expect stability rather than any big breakthrough, with trade disputes, Taiwan and the war in Iran all unresolved. Trade, global conflicts and AI are on the agenda.

Why it counts

What these two agree on sets tariffs and shipping costs for Chinese-made goods, which is the supply side of anything sourced cheaply online. A quiet summit means no sudden change; a bad one would move prices within weeks. This is a preview of a meeting that has not happened yet.

How credible is each label

  • News reportReporters writing down what happened. The most reliable kind.
  • Bank forecastA prediction from economists. Useful, but a guess about the future.
  • OpinionOne writer's argument. Read it as one side.
  • Market whisperWhat insiders are saying before anything is announced. Likely, not confirmed.
  • Gossip columnRear Window: insider gossip about powerful people. Entertaining, lightly sourced.

Words in today's paper

  • Futures A bet made today on what a price will be later, which is why traders can guess tomorrow's market.
  • Clearance rate Out of all the houses put up for auction, how many actually sold.
  • Secular stagnation The gloomy idea that a rich country's economy is stuck growing very slowly forever.
  • Productivity How much work one person can get done in an hour.
  • Intergenerational Report A government guess, made every few years, about how the country's money will look in forty years.
  • Take-private When investors buy every share of a listed company so it stops being owned by the public.
